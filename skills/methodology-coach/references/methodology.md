# Efficiency Methodology

> Catalog: 16 Execution + 19 Learning + 16 Thinking = 51 methods; 3 quick-reference tables; 7 combination chains; 6 case studies.

> A methodology solves "how to reliably take better action when time is limited and information is incomplete." It reduces execution uncertainty, but it does not replace judgment, nor does it promise results.
>
> This document organizes methods along three dimensions — **execution, learning, thinking** — and includes only three kinds of content: cognitive techniques with empirical support, management processes with practical consensus, and popular but weakly-evidenced thinking frameworks. Each method is tagged with an evidence grade; cases are illustrative only and do not constitute data evidence.
>
> **How to use**: first locate which link you're stuck on (Direction / Execution / Improvement / Learning / Communication), then look up by the map. Each category has a quick-reference table — Execution has "Planning Method Quick Reference", Learning has "Learning Division Quick Reference", Thinking has "Thinking Division Quick Reference". Every method states its **Boundary**, and **the boundary matters more than the method itself**.

**Evidence grading**

| Grade | Meaning                                                        | Usage                                   |
| ----- | -------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| S     | Supported by meta-analyses or multiple controlled experiments | Adopt directly, mind the applicability boundary |
| A     | Solid experiments or systematic reviews; effect depends on execution quality and domain | Adapt to context and verify periodically |
| B     | Theoretical basis and broad practical consensus                | Adapt to context and verify periodically |
| C     | Popular and logically coherent, but lacking direct evidence    | Use as a heuristic, never the sole basis |

## Build Judgment First: Four Filters

The more methods there are, the more you need a sieve. Before trusting any "viral methodology," run four questions:

| Filter | Question to ask | Failing form |
| ------ | --------------- | ------------ |
| Mechanism | Why might it work? | Only "tried it, it changed my life", no causal explanation |
| Action | What's my first step tomorrow morning? | All ideas, mindset, philosophy; nothing lands on a concrete action |
| Boundary | When does it fail? | Claims "works for everyone" or "just follow it blindly" |
| Cost | What do I give up to use it? | Only benefits, no mention of time cost or compliance burden |

**Four pseudo-evidence items to watch for**

1. **Learning Pyramid percentages** (5% lecture, 90% teaching others): no traceable original research; often misattributed to the NTL Institute. The directional conclusion (active output beats passive input) is usable; the numbers are not citable.
2. **"21 days to build a habit"**: originates from plastic surgeon Maltz's observation of patients' adjustment period, not an experiment. Lally et al. (2010) measured a median of about 66 days, with an individual range of 18–254 days.
3. **"10,000-hour rule"**: Ericsson's original text was about "deliberate practice"; Macnamara et al. (2014) meta-analysis shows practice explains only 4%–26% of performance variance.
4. **Ebbinghaus's precise review intervals** (5 min / 30 min / 12 h …): the forgetting curve itself is solid, but the "eight-cycle schedule" circulating online is a later fabrication, not from Ebbinghaus's original paper.

One deeper reminder: **"high likes / high saves" is a virality metric, not an evidence metric.** A post saved 100,000 times only proves it hit a nerve and reads well, not that it works. Treat viral posts as leads, not conclusions; treat a method as a hypothesis, and validate it with a month of your own data.

## Execution

### Planning Method Quick Reference

Choose a planning method by task structure, not by "which is best." The following are all practice-oriented tools, mostly B/C grade. If the problem isn't "how to schedule" but "no energy" or "can't focus," that's beyond planning methods — see Energy Management and Deep Work below.

| Name | Organizing dimension | Fits | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Time-based | Time order | Fixed schedule, depends on external collaboration | Sudden events easily disrupt the plan |
| Checklist | Importance | Many items, easy to miss | Easily ritualistic, done just to check off |
| Three-segment | Completion status | Needs a clear sense of progress | Inflexible, costly to adjust |
| OKR | Goal decomposition | Complex projects, needs multi-party alignment | Objectives and key results easily drift apart, becomes pro forma |
| Categorization | Eight facets | Needs a comprehensive review | Covering everything often achieves nothing |
| Four Quadrants | Priority vs. urgency | Mixed tasks, needs trade-offs | Long-term value items get squeezed out by urgent ones |
| Gantt chart | Date/progress | Parallel tasks, strong dependencies | Reflects time only, not quality |
| PDCA | Process loop | Needs continuous improvement | Process-ization breeds mental inertia |
| GTD | System externalization | Many tasks, multi-threaded | High maintenance, easily becomes list-tidying |
| Time Blocking | Calendar appointments | Needs protected focus blocks | Overpacking crashes; buffer required |
| Top-Three | Daily cap | Overlong list, decision paralysis | Unsuitable for crisis-response roles |
| Lyubishchev | Actual time spent | Wants to calibrate time estimation | Low compliance for full recording |

**Four generations of time management: know which layer you're on**

These methods are not parallel options — they sit in different generations, solving different layers. Skipping levels is the root cause of most time-management failures.

| Generation | Core | Representative tools | Solves |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Gen 1 | Don't forget | Memos, to-do lists | Externalize memory |
| Gen 2 | Boost efficiency | Calendar, schedules | Time allocation |
| Gen 3 | Effectiveness | Quadrants, goal-setting | Priority |
| Gen 4 | Meaning | Values, role balance, vision | Do the right things |

**Usage**: use Gen 1 to solve "forgetting", Gen 2 for "squeezing", Gen 3 for "choosing", then Gen 4 to answer "worth it?" Leaping straight to a life vision with no inbox at all only yields a beautiful piece of wastepaper.

### Time Management — Eisenhower Matrix (Four Quadrants) · B

**Logic**: replace "first come, first served" with "important × urgent", shifting energy from reactive firefighting to proactive fire prevention — urgent and important are often inversely related; first things first.

**Elements**: Q1 important & urgent (crisis zone, e.g. outages, deadlines) — handle now; Q2 important & not urgent (effectiveness zone, e.g. planning, learning, exercise) — the core investment of high performers; Q3 urgent & not important (distraction zone, e.g. irrelevant meetings, interruptive calls) — delegate or set boundaries; Q4 not urgent & not important (waste zone, e.g. mindless scrolling, trivia) — do less or drop.

**Application**: list all tasks → two questions per item (does it serve a long-term goal? is there an imminent deadline?) → execute by quadrant: Q1 Do, Q2 Decide, Q3 Delegate, Q4 Delete; reserve fixed time blocks for Q2.

**Example**: a project manager's Monday — Q1: finish the core of tomorrow's report in the morning; Q2: lock "learn AI" into an afternoon do-not-disturb block; Q3: route the complaint to the support lead, have a subordinate run the standup; Q4: cancel news-scrolling, batch email before leaving.

**Boundary**: without binding "important" to a concrete goal, the matrix degrades into subjective ranking; Q2 blocks must be protected as fixed calendar time, otherwise urgent items keep squeezing them out.

**Further reading**: [Time management — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_management)

### Pomodoro Technique · C

**Logic**: manage energy, not time — use short bursts of high-frequency focus plus regular rest to break a big task into manageable chunks, using urgency to boost focus and rest to avoid fatigue.

**Cycle**: one Pomodoro = 25 minutes of focus + 5 minutes of rest; every 4 Pomodoros take a 15–30 minute long break.

**Application**: pick a single task → set the timer → block distractions (phone silent, close unrelated tabs) → stop at the bell; on break stand up, drink water, look far away, don't scroll → record completed rounds.

**Example**: writing a quarterly report — without chunking, an afternoon of WeChat + news yields a few hundred words; switching to "just focus 25 minutes on the project background" gets the outline and first draft mostly done in 4 rounds.

**Boundary**: 25/5 has no empirically optimal value; adjust to 50/10 or 90 minutes by task. For deep reasoning tasks that need continuous thought, forced interruption may do more harm than good.

**Further reading**: [Pomodoro Technique — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pomodoro_Technique)

### Time Blocking · B

**Logic**: a to-do list answers only "what", not "when", so it keeps getting crowded out by urgent items. Time blocking translates tasks into calendar appointments, giving the plan a physical constraint.

**Application**: sketch next week on Sunday evening → plan each day each morning → place 1–3 ninety-minute deep blocks first (hardest tasks) → slot meetings, email, and errands into the remaining gaps → leave 20%–30% blank to absorb surprises → reconcile deviations nightly.

**Example**: same workload of "write proposal + answer email + weekly meeting", a list-based approach answers email first; time blocking instead: 9:00–10:30 write proposal (do-not-disturb), 10:30–11:00 batch messages, 14:00 meeting, 16:00 handle leftovers.

**Boundary**: overpacked blocks inevitably crash, and one crash makes you abandon the system. **Blank space is not waste, it's the system's buffer.** Roles that get interrupted constantly (support, on-call ops) shouldn't use whole-block scheduling; use "fragment list + batching" instead.

**Further reading**: [Sspai: The ultimate time-management solution (Life OS)](https://sspai.com/post/93919)

### Deep Work · B

**Logic**: high-value output = time × focus intensity. Fragmented work not only produces less, it degrades the ability to think deeply. Newport cites data that over 60% of knowledge workers replace deep work with shallow work.

**Four rules**: ① Work deeply — choose a depth philosophy (monastic / bimodal / rhythmic / journalistic), fix time and place, form a ritual; ② Embrace boredom — don't reach for your phone at every gap; ③ Quit social media — choose by "how much does this tool serve my core goal"; ④ Drain the shallows — plan every minute, quantify each activity's depth, set a shallow-work budget, fix a quitting time.

**Key concept: attention residue**. After an interruption, attention doesn't switch instantly — part of it stays on the previous task. Gloria Mark's research shows it takes about 23 minutes on average to return to the original task. **This is the mechanism behind "reply to one message, half an hour gone."**

**Boundary**: deep work suits cognitively intensive tasks — writing, coding, design, research — not collaboration-heavy roles that need high responsiveness. Forcing "4 hours of deep work daily" without reshaping the collaboration environment only produces guilt.

**Further reading**: [Zhihu: 3 hours beating a full day's work — Deep Work](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/62324764)

### Energy Management · B

**Logic**: time is a fixed constant, energy is a fluctuating variable. High performance comes from a consume–recover rhythm, not from extending work hours. Loehr and Schwartz, modeling athletes, propose four energy dimensions:

| Dimension | Source | Recovery |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Physical | Sleep, diet, exercise | 7–9h sleep, midday nap, interval aerobic exercise |
| Emotional | Relationships, meaning, positive experience | Social activities and hobbies, not binge-watching |
| Mental | Focus and creativity | Switch task type, leave blank space |
| Spiritual | Values and sense of purpose | Clarify the "why" |

**Application**: log a one-week energy curve (rate 1–5 every 2 hours) → find your peak window → put the hardest cognitive tasks in the peak window → schedule mechanical work or recovery in the troughs → use a fixed ritual (same time, same place, same action) to lower the startup cost.

**Boundary**: "scrolling = rest" is the biggest misconception — short video gives temporary numbness, not recovery, and raises the anxiety baseline over time. **Recovery quality depends on whether you've detached from work stimuli, not whether you're lying down.**

**Evidence**: Loehr & Schwartz's *The Power of Full Engagement* is based on athlete training and organizational interventions; the "recovery rhythm" is consistent with sleep-deprivation research, but the four-quadrant model itself lacks strict controlled experiments.

**Further reading**: [Zhihu: a full reading of The Power of Full Engagement](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/26573072)

### GTD (Getting Things Done) · B

**Logic**: the main source of anxiety isn't having too much to do, it's unfinished items continuously occupying working memory (Zeigarnik effect). Externalizing everything into a trusted system lets the brain return to "mind like water" and handle only the present.

**Five steps**: ① Capture — every thought, task, and commitment goes into the inbox; record fast, don't judge; ② Clarify — should I do it? What outcome do I want? What's the next action? ③ Organize — file into five lists: next actions, waiting-for, calendar, someday/maybe, reference; ④ Review — 5 minutes daily on the calendar and action list, a weekly full review to empty the inbox and update projects; ⑤ Engage — choose an action by context, time, energy, and priority.

**Key move**: rewrite a fuzzy task as "date + verb + event + person", e.g. "call Jack before Wednesday to confirm the report spec". **The next action must be a physical action you can take immediately, not "advance the project".**

**Boundary**: GTD is a heavy system with high maintenance cost. With a small task volume, jumping straight into GTD turns into "tidying the list of lists." Beginners should start with just "inbox + daily review".

**Further reading**: [Sspai: from methodology to practice, mastering GTD](https://sspai.com/post/57851)

### Top-Three Tasks / Eat the Frog · C

**Logic**: an overlong list triggers decision paralysis, and people tend to do the easy ones first. Forcing a choice of only three (at least one being the "ugliest frog") puts limited self-control on the highest-value items.

**Application**: the night before, write tomorrow's three most important tasks → do the first one in the morning before touching messages → open the inbox only after finishing → carry unfinished items over, never stack them on top.

**Boundary**: three is a heuristic, not a law. Crisis-response roles need a "insertion channel" or reality will repeatedly smack you until you abandon it.

**Further reading**: [Brian Tracy — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian_Tracy)

### Lyubishchev Time Tracking · C

**Logic**: humans are highly unreliable at perceiving time — "thought I was busy all day" and "actually produced two effective hours" can both be true. Log time expenditures item by item to calibrate estimates with data.

**Application**: record "time block + task" at 15–30 minute granularity → summarize and categorize weekly → compare estimate vs. actual → correct the next estimate.

**Boundary**: **minute-precise full-day logging has very low compliance** — most people can't last two weeks. A more realistic approach is to track only Pomodoro / deep-block investment, not a 24-hour ledger. Its core value is training estimation, not leaving a perfect account book.

**Source**: Granin, *This Strange Life of Mine*.

### Atomic Habits / Tiny Habits · B

**Logic**: a habit is the loop "cue → craving → response → reward". The highest-leverage way to change a habit is not willpower but identity and environment design.

**Four rules**: make it obvious (cue) — put the book by the bed, the phone in another room; make it attractive (craving) — bundle the thing you want to do with something you enjoy; make it easy (response) — the two-minute rule, "just read one paragraph", "just do five jumping jacks"; make it satisfying (reward) — tick it off immediately, track the streak.

**Application**: first fix the identity ("I'm someone who writes every day") → pick a two-minute version of the action → attach it after an existing habit → design the environment so the right action is the path of least resistance → track the streak; miss one day, don't make it up; two weeks off, restart.

**Boundary**: the 1% compound-interest story is compelling, but habit gains are not linear — there are plateaus and stalls; treating "1% better every day" as a promise breeds frustration. Habits suit long-term behaviors in stable environments, not complex tasks that need frequent judgment.

**Evidence**: Clear, *Atomic Habits*; Guise, *Mini Habits*; Lally et al. (2010) habit-formation study (median ~66 days).

**Further reading**: [Zhihu: Atomic Habits 4 rules (with checklist)](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/1921653262324569888)

### Personal SOP · B

**Logic**: the cost of repetitive work isn't execution, it's re-deciding every time. An SOP pins down "how to judge, in what order, to what standard", freeing cognitive resources for things that actually need judgment.

**Application**: identify high-frequency repetitive tasks (weekly reports, incident triage, interviews, publishing) → write the steps, decision branches, checklist, and common pitfalls → run it once while logging friction points → iterate to v2 → re-check quarterly whether it still applies.

**Boundary**: SOPs suit tasks with stable processes and verifiable outcomes; creative work that gets SOP'd degenerates into formalism. The biggest risk isn't having none, it's "written but never read, read but never updated".

**Further reading**: [Bilibili: work like the rich, build your own SOP](https://www.bilibili.com/opus/1064142438969376774)

### PDCA · B

**Logic**: turn a one-off task into an accumulating improvement loop — reject linear thinking, use the "Plan-Do-Check-Act" spiral to solidify experience into standards and carry problems into the next round.

**Cycle**: Plan (analyze the current state, find the main cause, set measures) → Do (execute the plan, supervise to keep the action undistorted) → Check (compare results against goals with data) → Act (solidify what worked into standards, summarize lessons from failures, carry leftovers into the next cycle).

**Example**: incident governance — P: set "cut the failure rate 50%" plus a review mechanism and automated inspection; D: roll out code review, monitoring, and connection-pool fixes; C: failure rate drops 30% but logic-error alarms rise; A: solidify connection-pool parameters as an ops standard, and route logic-error governance into the next cycle targeting unit-test coverage.

**Boundary**: the most common failure is skipping the data-driven Check, collapsing the loop into "Plan-Do-Plan again"; Act's output must be a standard or a next-round list, otherwise the loop isn't closed.

**Further reading**: [PDCA — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PDCA)

### SMART Criteria · B

**Logic**: translate a fuzzy wish into a verifiable action contract — an imprecisely-defined goal is just an emotional slogan; five-dimension enforcement makes it executable, trackable, and judgeable.

**Elements**: Specific (what exactly to do), Measurable (to what extent counts as done), Attainable (challenging but achievable with effort), Relevant (tied to a bigger strategy or long-term goal), Time-bound (a clear deadline or milestone).

**Application**: draft the goal → interrogate each dimension (which concrete thing? what number proves it? reachable? tied to the core goal? latest completion date?) → add "what's step one this week" → fold into weekly/monthly review, correct on drift.

**Example**: "study tech better this year" rewrites to "by Dec 31, read the Spring core container source and publish 6 analysis posts, serving the framework-principles capability needed for promotion, ~5 hours a week; step one this week: set up the source-debug environment and walk through the BeanDefinition loading flow."

**Boundary**: "measurable" and "time-bound" are not necessary conditions for effectiveness; overly narrow quantified goals induce short-sightedness and data gaming.

**Evidence**: Locke & Latham's goal-setting theory supports specific, difficult, feedback-rich goals; Ordóñez et al. (2009) argue the side effects of overly narrow goals.

**Further reading**: [SMART criteria — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SMART_criteria)

### Pareto Principle (80/20) · B

**Logic**: output and input are non-linearly distributed — about 80% of results come from about 20% of causes; the key to efficiency isn't finishing everything, it's identifying the vital few and piling resources onto them.

**Application**: list everything → quantify and rank each item's contribution → lock the top 20% → tilt golden hours, best people, and budget toward them first → delegate, batch, downgrade, or drop the long tail (the trivial many).

**Example**: B2B SaaS customer success — averaging effort across 120 customers runs you ragged; pulling the billing shows 22 customers contribute 78% of renewals; tilt senior managers and one-on-one service entirely toward those 22, move the long tail to self-serve docs and batch ops; head renewals rise from 70% to 92%, upsells double.

**Boundary**: 80/20 is descriptive experience, not a universal law, and not an exact 8:2; the real distribution may be a power law or long tail — measure the ratio, don't apply it blindly.

**Further reading**: [Pareto principle — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pareto_principle)

### Pre-mortem · A

**Logic**: at the start of a plan, assume the goal has already failed and work backward to the causes, bypassing optimism bias and group silence.

**Application**: gather stakeholders → set a point in time where "the project has failed" → each person independently writes failure causes (no criticism, exhaust first, rank later) → merge, dedupe, rank → pre-install countermeasures and monitoring signals for high-probability causes.

**Boundary**: the output is a risk list that must be wired into decisions and owners, otherwise it's just a paper drill; it pre-rehearses only an already-chosen plan — comparing candidate plans belongs to the decision matrix.

**Evidence**: introduced by Klein in *Harvard Business Review*; the "prospective hindsight" experiment shows assuming an event has occurred lets people identify ~30% more causes (Mitchell et al., 1989).

**Further reading**: [Pre-mortem — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-mortem)

### Decision Matrix · B

**Logic**: make multi-criteria judgment explicit, reducing the influence of intuition, rhetoric, and rank.

**Application**: list options → set criteria → assign weights → score each → weighted sum → run a sensitivity check (does changing weights flip the conclusion).

**Boundary**: weights are subjective; the conclusion is only trustworthy while the criteria are stable. Weights must be fixed in advance — adjusting weights afterward to flatter a preset conclusion is the most common failure. It can't replace validating key assumptions.

**Further reading**: [Decision matrix — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decision_matrix)

### After-Action Review (AAR) · B

**Logic**: replace blame arguments with fact alignment, turning experience into reusable rules.

**Four questions**: what was expected → what actually happened → why the difference → what to change next time.

**Discipline**: align on data and facts first; address the issue, not the person; conclusions must land on executable changes, otherwise the review is void.

**Boundary**: starting a review before facts align degrades into blame arguments or a witch hunt; stating only conclusions without owners and changes is the same as no review.

**Division of labor with PDCA**: AAR handles a single event or project closeout, PDCA handles the ongoing loop; AAR's conclusions feed PDCA to solidify into standards and roll into the next cycle.

**Lightweight variants: KISS / KPT · C** — AAR's four questions lean toward "aligning cognition"; KISS/KPT lean toward "behavioral output", suited to high-frequency daily and weekly reviews. KISS = Keep / Improve / Stop / Start; KPT = Keep / Problem / Try. **Boundary**: the fatal flaw of lightweight reviews is "write but never do" — every conclusion must bind to a verifiable action next week, otherwise it's just an emotion diary; deep project reviews should still use AAR + PDCA.

**Further reading**: [After-action review — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/After-action_review), [Zhihu: 6 methods for daily/weekly/monthly/yearly/project reviews](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/2086027460978676274)

## Learning

### Learning Division Quick Reference

Each method owns one link; locate the problem first, then pick — they're not interchangeable: understanding → Feynman; memory → spaced repetition and recall; application → interleaving and deliberate practice; input → SQ3R and syntopical reading; curation → Zettelkasten; output → output-driven input.

| You want to solve | Use |
| --- | --- |
| Understand it but can't explain it | Feynman Technique |
| Can't remember, forget fast | Spaced repetition (timing) + active recall (retrieval) |
| Can do it but can't do it well | Deliberate practice |
| Can't transfer to a new problem type | Interleaved practice |
| Practicing hard but don't know what's wrong | Feedback |
| Read a textbook and forget it | SQ3R |
| Led around by a single author | Syntopical reading |
| Notes sit unused, can't write anything | Zettelkasten (slip-box) |
| Lots of input, zero output | Output-driven input |
| Notes become copying, can't review | Cornell Notes |
| Can't retain structural/process knowledge | Dual coding |
| Can't memorize an ordered list | Method of loci |
| Learning scattered, can't break through | Simon method (focused assault) |
| Knowledge fragmented, won't form a net | Knowledge tree / networked learning (C) |
| Training never transfers to the job | Action learning (L = P + Q) |
| Materials getting messier | Second Brain / PARA (C) |
| Have time but no energy | Energy management + flow |
| Only input, no output, forget after learning | Learning Pyramid (directional reference, C) |

### Feynman Technique · B

**Logic**: use output to force input — "knowing" and "understanding" are different; the high-intensity output of "teaching" forces the brain to simplify and reorganize knowledge, quickly exposing blind spots and converting short-term memory into deeply retrievable long-term memory.

**Application**: pick a topic (Concept) → explain it in plain words to someone who knows nothing (Teach; if you stall or must borrow jargon, you don't really get it) → return to the source for the stuck points (Review) → strip jargon and simplify with analogies (Simplify, e.g. voltage as water pressure, compound interest as a snowball) → actually teach someone or record yourself; mastery means the other person can understand and restate it.

**Example**: explaining an API to business people — as a restaurant waiter: the menu is the interface doc, ordering is the request, the kitchen is the backend, serving is the returned data; this internalizes the API's essence of "mediation, decoupling, standardization".

**Boundary**: teaching an imagined audience is weaker than a real audience's follow-up questions; the four-step form isn't Feynman's own, it's a later popular summary.

**Further reading**: [Learning by teaching — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Learning_by_teaching)

### Spaced Repetition · S

**Logic**: fight the forgetting curve — review at the "about to forget" tipping point to solidify short-term memory into long-term memory at minimal cost.

**Elements**: review intervals lengthen gradually (e.g. 1 day, 3 days, 1 week, 1 month) rather than all at once; each review reinforces the memory trace — timing matters more than total duration.

**Application**: make atomic flashcards (front = question/cue, back = answer) → schedule with Anki, Quizlet, etc., self-test new cards immediately → auto-schedule the next review by familiarity (again/hard/good/easy), the harder the more frequent → clear all due reviews daily.

**Example**: preparing for a CPA exam on journal entries and tax rules — make cards from day one, review during commutes and lunch, each item reappears on days 1/3/7/15 until mastered, leaving only a light review before the exam.

**Boundary**: good for facts, terms, and language; not for understanding and transfer; don't copy whole passages onto cards.

**Evidence**: Cepeda et al. (2006) meta-analysis of 300+ experiments; rated high-utility by Dunlosky et al. (2013).

**Further reading**: [Spaced repetition — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spaced_repetition)

### Active Recall · S

**Logic**: the essence of learning isn't input, it's retrieval — forcing the brain to actively search memory strengthens neural connections, improving retention strength and retrieval speed, far outperforming passive rereading.

**Application**: close the material right after a section → self-question (what key points did this cover? what's the definition? how does it connect to what I know?) → blank-page reproduction (redraw a mind map / flowchart or write down everything you remember) → reopen and check, marking omissions and errors in a different color → retest the gaps.

**Example**: studying a machine-learning article — rereading and highlighting, then being unable to state the core idea when the book closes; instead, write on a blank page "which algorithms were covered? their pros/cons? how do their use cases differ?" then check — harder, but actually learned.

**Boundary**: must pair with feedback, otherwise it only exposes gaps without correcting errors; difficulty should sit slightly above the current level.

**Evidence**: Roediger & Karpicke (2006); rated high-utility by Dunlosky et al. (2013).

**Further reading**: [Testing effect — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Testing_effect)

### Deliberate Practice · A

**Logic**: practice weak sub-skills at high intensity with immediate feedback, instead of repeating what you already can do.

**Application**: decompose the capability → locate the weakest link → design a targeted task (clear goal, outside the comfort zone, with a reference benchmark) → get immediate feedback → correct and repeat.

**Example**: preparing for a technical certification — a mock exam every 2 weeks; wrong answers locate the weakest sub-skill, which becomes the next round's focus, rather than redoing question types already mastered.

**Boundary**: not the "10,000-hour rule"; duration is not a sufficient condition; without feedback, practice solidifies errors.

**Evidence**: Ericsson et al. (1993); Macnamara et al. (2014) meta-analysis: practice explains only 4%–26% of performance variance, with large domain differences.

**Further reading**: [Deliberate practice — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deliberate_practice)

### Interleaved Practice · A

**Logic**: mix different problem types and topics, forcing the brain to re-judge the strategy each time, improving discrimination and transfer.

**Application**: switch from blocked practice to mixed → randomize by type → attribute errors immediately.

**Boundary**: a short-term drop in accuracy is normal; if the foundation isn't built yet, do blocked practice first, then interleave.

**Evidence**: Dunlosky et al. (2013) systematic review.

**Further reading**: [Dunlosky et al. 2013 review](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266)

### Feedback · A

**Logic**: feedback is one of the largest variables affecting learning; its value depends on pointing at the task and process, not at the person.

**Application**: align on the standard first → give specific, timely, actionable information (three questions: what's the goal → where are we now → what's next) → land on the next action → follow up on the result.

**Boundary**: evaluative feedback (praising talent, criticizing the person) is weak or even harmful; in complex tasks, delayed feedback sometimes works better.

**Evidence**: Hattie & Timperley (2007), The Power of Feedback.

**Further reading**: [Hattie & Timperley (2007), The Power of Feedback](https://doi.org/10.3102/003465430298487)

### SQ3R Reading Method · B

**Logic**: reading's inefficiency comes from passive word-scanning. SQ3R turns reading into retrieval practice via "question first, find answers, recite after reading".

**Five steps**: ① Survey — look at titles, TOC, figures, first/last paragraphs, and section summaries to build a map; ② Question — turn each heading into a question ("what is the nature of power?"); ③ Read — read to answer, mark only main ideas, don't highlight whole pages; ④ Recite — close the book after a section and answer your questions in your own words; ⑤ Review — revisit notes and questions regularly, self-test with answers covered.

**Example**: reading a section on distributed transactions — first write "what's 2PC's biggest pain?" and "why does TCC need compensation logic?" as questions, then read to find answers, then close the book and recite, returning to the source where stuck.

**Boundary**: SQ3R suits textbooks and professional material that are well-structured and must be mastered; skip it for novels, leisure reading, and quick lookup. It's a different packaging of the same mechanism as "active recall + spaced repetition" — don't build two duplicate systems.

**Evidence**: Robinson (1946), widely used in university reading programs; consistent with retrieval-practice and generation-effect research.

**Further reading**: [Stanford CTL: SQ3R Reading Method](https://ctl.stanford.edu/students/reading-efficacy-sq3r-method)

### Syntopical Reading · B

**Logic**: a single book gives one author's perspective; syntopical reading gives a full landscape of one problem. It's the highest of the four reading levels in *How to Read a Book*.

**Application**: fix the question first (not the book list) → find 5–10 relevant books → use inspectional reading to filter relevant chapters → build a neutral glossary (different authors name the same concept differently) → list the issues around the question → make a comparison table of each author's position on each issue → form your own judgment.

**Boundary**: high cost; unsuitable for the entry stage of "quickly getting to know a new field". **Read one good survey first, then enter syntopical reading.** The output should be a "position-comparison table + your own conclusion", not a pile of book notes.

**Source**: Mortimer Adler, *How to Read a Book*.

### Zettelkasten (Slip-box) Method · B

**Logic**: the value of notes isn't storage, it's connection and reproduction. Luhmann wrote dozens of books from ~90,000 cards, not by memory but by letting notes cite each other and naturally grow themes.

| Type | Purpose | Lifespan |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fleeting note | Capture an idea, no formatting | Process within 24–48h |
| Literature note | A response to a book/article, keep the source | Long-term |
| Permanent (atomic) note | One idea per card, in your own words, stands alone | Long-term asset |
| Expression note | Several atomic notes assembled into an article/report/talk | One-off output |

**Core principle**: **composition beats aggregation** — atomized knowledge recombines freely across contexts. Add bidirectional links so "what you write today" meets "what you wrote three years ago".

**Boundary**: the Zettelkasten is easiest to turn into "an exquisite collection folder". **If three months pass with no expression note, the system is idling.** It benefits writers and researchers most; for people who only need retrieval, it's over-engineering.

**Evidence**: Luhmann's slip-box practice; the mechanism is consistent with generation effect, elaboration, and spaced retrieval, but lacks controlled experiments; graded B rather than A.

**Further reading**: [Zhihu: Second Brain — the Zettelkasten in the AI era](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/2072621196781401118)

### Output-Driven Input · B

**Logic**: fix the deliverable first (article, talk, project, course), then work backward to what you need to learn. It solves three problems at once: filtering input (don't learn the irrelevant), forcing processing (generation effect), and forming feedback (real readers/users).

**Application**: fix a deliverable due in 2–4 weeks → work backward to the knowledge gaps → learn only the gaps → write/build as you learn → publish and get feedback → turn feedback into the next round of input.

**Boundary**: easy to slide into "updating for the sake of updating", producing lots of low-quality content. **The deliverable must have real users and a real standard**, otherwise it's self-congratulation. Its division of labor with Feynman: Feynman is "explain it clearly", output-driven input is "make it real".

**Further reading**: [Zhihu: don't just read and learn, output heavily](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/1890135062843982858)

### Cornell Notes · C

**Logic**: upgrade note-taking from a copyist to a processing plant — a fixed page layout forces the three-stage deep processing of "record → distill → recite" in the moment, making notes a directly reviewable, self-testable knowledge asset.

**Elements**: right note column (content, facts, formulas, examples), left cue column (keywords and core questions — the self-test question bank), bottom summary area (one or two sentences summarizing the page); paired with the 5 Rs: Record, Reduce, Recite, Reflect, Review.

**Application**: record key points in the note column with short phrases and symbols, don't transcribe verbatim → distill into the cue column as questions the same day → cover the note column and restate from the cues in your own words → write connections to prior knowledge and real application, plus a one-sentence bottom summary → periodically review the cue column and summary.

**Example**: a talk on distributed transactions — note column briefly captures the mechanics and pitfalls of 2PC/TCC/Saga/local-message-table; cue column distills questions ("2PC's biggest pain?", "why does TCC write compensation?"); summary area: "distributed transactions have no silver bullet — it's a trade-off among consistency, performance, and migration cost"; before an interview, cover the right and test from the left.

**Boundary**: the layout itself lacks direct experimental evidence; the genuinely effective ingredient is the retrieval practice of "distill questions + cover-and-self-test".

**Further reading**: [Cornell Notes — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornell_Notes)

### Dual Coding · B

**Logic**: store the same information in two codes — verbal and visual — gaining an extra retrieval path; when the verbal cue breaks, the image cue can fill in; the two paths back each other up and reinforce each other.

**Elements**: based on Dual Coding Theory: the verbal system (words, numbers) and the imagery system (images, space); represent the same knowledge in "text + diagram" and store/retrieve them together.

**Application**: read for verbal representation → actively translate into a picture or diagram (flowchart, structure diagram, timeline, scene); generating it yourself is key → co-annotate and physically place text and image together for a strong bond → close the material and redraw from memory; the part you can't draw is the blind spot → review by testing the image with the text and vice versa.

**Example**: memorizing the TCP three-way handshake — the verbal side works out the three message fields and sequence numbers; the image side imagines a phone call ("SYN: can you hear me?", "SYN+ACK: I can hear you, can you hear me?", "ACK: I hear you, let's talk") and draws an arrow sequence diagram labeling the messages; when a field is forgotten, walking through the picture explains it.

**Boundary**: biggest gains for spatial, structural, and process knowledge; imaginary encoding of purely verbal material is rated low-utility by Dunlosky et al. (2013).

**Further reading**: [Dual-coding theory — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual-coding_theory)

### Method of Loci (Memory Palace) · B

**Logic**: spatial memory is one of the strongest human memory systems. Anchor the information to be remembered onto a familiar route, replacing "reciting" with "walking through", essentially adding a spatial retrieval cue — the same family as dual coding.

**Application**: choose a familiar route (home → subway → office) → place an exaggerated, dynamic, absurd image at each location → recall in route order → walk through it periodically to consolidate.

**Example**: memorizing five speech points — a burning clock at the door (opening), reports piled on the shoe cabinet (data), a collapsed sofa in the living room (problem), the plan on the dining table (solution), a rising sun on the balcony (vision).

**Boundary**: good for ordered lists (speech points, exam items, shopping lists), not for understanding; building images is time-costly, **only worthwhile when "recall in exact order" matters**.

**Evidence**: the method of loci in memory competition and cognitive psychology; stable effect, narrow applicability.

**Further reading**: [Method of loci — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Method_of_loci)

### Learning Pyramid · C

**Logic**: participation determines retention — a pyramid from passive reception to active output; the more "exhausting" the learning, the stronger the retention; "teaching" is the highest-leverage "learning".

**Elements**: passive learning (lecture, reading, audio-visual, demonstration) retains little; active learning (group discussion, hands-on practice, teaching others / immediate application) retains much.

**Application**: check whether you're stuck at attending lectures, reading, watching videos → debate contested points with a group or colleagues after each module → land knowledge in real output (write a small program to learn programming, solve a real problem to learn theory) → blog, share, teach others → route high-value knowledge through the full chain "listen → discuss → practice → teach".

**Example**: mastering prompt engineering — a dozen saved tutorials, still a blank mind two weeks later; switch to debating "how to design stable structured output" in a group, practice on a real task (use prompts to batch-convert Java code into unit tests), and a month later give a 30-minute internal share and write a blog.

**Boundary**: the common percentage version ("5% lecture, 10% reading … 90% teaching others") has no traceable original research (often misattributed to the NTL Institute) — do not cite it. The usable conclusion is to upgrade passive reception to discussion, practice, and teaching — a directional judgment, not a precise model.

**Further reading**: [Learning pyramid — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Learning_pyramid)

### Simon Method · C

**Logic**: the learning view of Herbert Simon (Nobel in economics, Turing Award) is summarized as: concentrate attention on one narrow domain and keep attacking it for a limited time, rather than spreading thin. Often packaged as "master a subject in 6 months".

**Application**: pick a narrow-enough domain → break it into verifiable knowledge blocks → set a 3–6 month concentrated cycle → fixed high-density daily investment → verify with output (notes, projects, explanations).

**Boundary**: "master any subject in 6 months" is a propagation exaggeration, **with no controlled experiment supporting that number**. The credible part: focus + intensity + long duration beats scattered investment. It's fundamentally an "attention budget" problem, not magic.

**Further reading**: [Simon Method (popular book)](https://book.douban.com/subject/36810902/)

### Knowledge Tree / Networked Learning · C

**Logic**: knowledge points memorized in isolation can't be retrieved. Hang each new piece of knowledge onto an existing structure; thinking repeatedly about the relationships between pieces during "classification" and "systematization" builds a retrievable network.

**Application**: record promptly (which branch does this knowledge belong to?) → organize periodically (rebuild categories, map with mind maps) → keep sinking down (distill methods into more fundamental principles) → check the framework for overlaps and gaps with MECE.

**Boundary**: framework-first easily becomes "classifying for the sake of classifying". **A beginner's biggest problem is that the framework itself is wrong** — so early on, directly borrow a mature framework from the field (a classic textbook's table of contents), then progressively adapt it. Also, the "tree" metaphor implies hierarchy, but real knowledge is more like a network — **don't sacrifice cross-domain connections for the tree's tidiness**.

**Further reading**: [Zhihu: Knowledge Tree — the ultimate learning method](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/107023704)

### Action Learning · B

**Logic**: Revans proposed **L = P + Q** — learning (L) equals existing knowledge (P) plus the ability to ask deep questions (Q). Management ability isn't taught in classrooms; it grows from solving real problems in team collaboration.

**Application**: form a 4–8 person group → each person brings a real, unsolved problem → present → questions only, no advice → reflect on assumptions → form an action → bring back the result next time.

**Boundary**: depends on high-quality questioning and psychological safety. If it turns into a "leader's critique session" or "experience sharing", it fails. It suits managers and complex problems, not skill training (which needs deliberate practice).

**Further reading**: [Action learning — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_learning)

### Second Brain / PARA · C

**Logic**: the failure of information management isn't collection, it's not organizing by "actionability". PARA replaces infinite nested folders with four top-level containers: Projects (a goal and a deadline), Areas (standards to maintain long-term), Resources (topics for possible future use), Archives (inactive items from the above).

**Application**: judge each new item by "which project/area does it serve?" → file into the matching container → archive when a project ends → sweep the resource area monthly.

**Boundary**: PARA is an organizing scheme, not a learning method. **Collecting without processing turns the second brain into a second landfill.** It must pair with "atomic notes + output", otherwise it's just a tidier warehouse.

**Further reading**: [Tiago Forte, *Building a Second Brain*](https://www.buildingasecondbrain.com/)

### Flow · B

**Logic**: Csikszentmihalyi's three flow conditions — clear goals, immediate feedback, and a match between challenge and skill. In flow, attention is highly concentrated, time perception warps, and efficiency rises markedly.

**Application**: set task difficulty to "a stretch, just reachable" (slightly above current ability) → engineer immediate feedback (check output every 25 minutes) → clear distractions → give a clear entry ritual → allow the first 10 minutes to be imperfect.

**Boundary**: "quickly entering flow" tricks are mostly C-grade experience. Flow is the result of conditions being met, not a state you can command directly. Treating it as a KPI makes you anxious about "why am I not in flow yet".

**Further reading**: [Flow — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_(psychology))

## Thinking

### Thinking Division Quick Reference

Thinking methods solve "how to think clearly and express clearly". Locate the sticking point first, then pick the framework — don't use one hammer on every nail.

| You want to solve | Use |
| --- | --- |
| Blank mind on a hard problem | Structured thinking (top-down / bottom-up) |
| Others can't follow your point | Pyramid Principle (conclusion first) + SCQA as opening |
| Analysis has overlap or gaps | MECE |
| Only analogize, afraid to rebuild | First Principles |
| Fix symptoms, repeat mistakes | 5 Whys |
| The more you fix, the worse it gets | Systems thinking (loops / delays) |
| Reporting data with no conclusion | Sky–Rain–Umbrella (fact–interpretation–action) |
| Can't say why you're doing this | Golden Circle (Why-How-What) |
| Meeting where everyone talks past each other | Six Thinking Hats |
| Choosing options by gut feeling | Decision Matrix (see Execution) |
| Can't see internal/external landscape | SWOT + PEST / 3C / Five Forces |
| Fear of black swans, want antifragility | Barbell strategy (C-grade, heuristic only) |

### Structured Thinking · B

**Logic**: when facing a complex problem, find a structure before filling in content. The brain can't process scattered information but is excellent at structured information. If a ready framework exists, apply it top-down; if not, induce one bottom-up.

**Bottom-up four steps**: brainstorm exhaustively → link and group → distill the structure with MECE → supplement and refine.

**Daily training**: ① accumulate structures — when you see a good theory, think of three application scenarios and file them; ② write — one article a week in a structured way, or at least build the skeleton; ③ explain a topic — 15–20 minutes daily, argue a topic in a structured way.

**Boundary**: structuring presupposes enough material. **Forcing a structure with insufficient material yields a beautiful empty shell.** It excels at "making the complex clear", not "creating from nothing" — the latter needs divergence and association.

**Evidence**: a 50k-like Zhihu answer and consulting practice consensus; cognitive-load research supports "chunking lowers burden".

**Further reading**: [Zhihu 50k likes: how the truly capable think](https://news.mbalib.com/story/245492)

### Pyramid Principle · B

**Logic**: unify "thinking" and "expressing" into the same tree — think bottom-up, express top-down. The brain can't process scattered information but handles structured information well.

**Four principles**: conclusion first, higher-level summarizes lower, group like with like, order logically.

**Three substructures**: an SCQA-style opening story, a vertical question/answer dialogue, and horizontal deduction or induction. SCQA is the Pyramid's opening substructure and can be used as a standalone opener; but in the "can't express clearly" scenario, the primary method is still the Pyramid Principle, with SCQA as its opening component — no conflict with "one primary method per stage".

**Boundary**: suits persuasive expression and proposal reporting, not exploratory discussion (conclusion-first too early kills possibilities). **Conclusion-first doesn't mean a single conclusion** — complex problems should give "main conclusion + conditions".

**Further reading**: [Zhihu: the Pyramid Principle in one diagram](https://www.zhihu.com/tardis/zm/ans/2594361780)

### MECE · B

**Logic**: Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive. It ensures analysis "thinks neither too much nor too little" — the precondition for each level of the pyramid to stand.

**Application**: fix the decomposition purpose first (by time? by customer? by process?) → pick one dimension → check overlap (can one item fall into two groups?) → check gaps (anything that fits nowhere?) → name it (the name must summarize everything in the group).

**Boundary**: MECE is a checklist for thinking quality, not the goal. In reality many problems can't be fully exhausted; falling back to "Collectively Exhaustive" or "Mutually Exclusive" alone still has value. **Manufacturing a false classification to satisfy MECE is worse than not being MECE.**

**Further reading**: [MECE principle — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MECE_principle)

### SCQA · B

**Logic**: rebuild the audience's question with "situation–complication–question–answer" so your conclusion lands exactly on their question.

| Element | Role |
| --- | --- |
| S Situation | Establish shared background, build safety |
| C Complication | Introduce change or conflict, create tension |
| Q Question | Turn the tension into a clear question |
| A Answer | Give your conclusion |

**Four variants**: standard SCQA (steady), CSA anxiety-first (crisis communication), QSCA confidence-first (lead with the question), ASC answer-first (time-pressed).

**Boundary**: SCQA is an opening structure, not a whole-document structure. A too-long background loses the audience; re-telling the conflict when they already know it wastes time.

**Source**: Barbara Minto, *The Pyramid Principle*; *The McKinsey Mind*.

### 5W2H · B

**Logic**: use seven fixed dimensions to force de-fuzzification, dissecting the topic without dead angles, turning a vague idea into an executable, quantifiable, accountable plan.

**Elements**: also called the seven-question analysis — What (purpose, content, or the task itself), Why (cause, motivation, necessity — whether it's worth doing), Who (owner, participants, users, or affected parties), When (start/end, key nodes, best timing), Where (location, stage, or entry point), How (method, steps, flow), How Much (cost/time input and expected output quantity/quality/standard).

**Application**: define the topic → answer all seven questions one by one → review for gaps (any fuzzy answer marks an improvement area) → integrate into a landable plan.

**Example**: assigning a database optimization task — "it's a bit slow, optimize it" will surely bounce; the seven questions pin it down: cut the order-query API P99 from over 500ms to under 200ms, effort ≤ 3 person-days (How Much), focus on the orders main table (Where), Xiao Wang leads with DBA support (Who), root-cause report Friday and go-live next Wednesday (When), first locate the Top 5 slow SQL, then look at execution plans, index first and refactor if needed (How).

**Boundary**: it's a checklist at heart; its value is in execution discipline. Why is the most commonly skipped, yet it decides whether the plan is worth doing.

**Further reading**: [Five Ws — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_Ws)

### STAR · B

**Logic**: based on "past behavior is the best predictor of future performance", use structured narrative to turn fuzzy personal capability into objectively assessable behavioral evidence.

**Application**: Situation — 1–2 sentences of background, only what's needed; Task — the core problem or goal, defining the scope of responsibility; Action — "judgment → action → collaboration", about 60%–70% of the length, first-person "I" to highlight personal contribution; Result — end with concrete data echoing the goal, using percentages, amounts, and time to persuade.

**Example**: an interview answer on "handling sudden risk" — S: three days before the promo, the core server overloaded and went down; T: recover within 24 hours and set a compensation plan; A: located the traffic-spike root cause, decided on scaling and query optimization, led the design of the "100-off-30" coupon; R: recovered in 12 hours, same-day GMV exceeded expectations by 15%, 40% redemption rate, complaints down to 2%.

**Boundary**: STAR is an expression template, not a structured interview; self-reports inflate easily and need probing details and counterfactuals to verify.

**Further reading**: [What is the STAR method?](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/485385589), [STAR in data-PM interviews](https://www.woshipm.com/pmd/5517853.html), [Situation, task, action, result — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Situation,_task,_action,_result)

### Mind Mapping · C

**Logic**: simulate the brain's radial thinking — turn linear, abstract information into a nonlinear, visual structure, using keywords, colors, images, and branches to clarify thinking, build systems, and spark association.

**Application**: write the core topic in the center → draw a few thick curves outward as main branches (core subtopics) → extend thinner twigs for details, cases, or ideas → write only one keyword per line to force distillation, color-code branches, add icons for memory.

**Example**: reading *The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People* — center "seven habits", seven main branches (e.g. "be proactive", "begin with the end in mind") each extending twigs for definitions, key actions, and cases; one diagram shows the whole book's framework and inter-habit relations.

**Boundary**: good for divergence and organizing, not for rigorous reasoning or linear argument; its "improves memory and creativity" claim lacks strong evidence.

**Further reading**: [Mind map — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mind_map)

### SWOT Analysis · B

**Logic**: put internal and external reality on the same table — a calm four-cell inventory of "internal (strengths/weaknesses) × external (opportunities/threats)", turning a static list into a dynamic "attack / defend / transform" strategy.

**Elements**: S strengths (internal: good at, leading, unique resources/capabilities), W weaknesses (internal: lacking, lagging, constraints), O opportunities (external: favorable, leverageable trends), T threats (external: risks and rival moves that may harm you); TOWS cross: SO attack, WO improve, ST defend, WT avoid.

**Application**: build a 2×2 grid → list internal S/W objectively with facts and data, scan external O/T across industry, policy, competition, technology → keep only the 3–5 most critical items per cell, delete vague, undifferentiated statements → cross-generate strategy (SO use strengths to ride the trend, WO fix weaknesses to grab opportunities, ST use strengths to hedge risks, WT avoid where weakness meets risk) → translate into a plan with owners and a timeline.

**Example**: a senior architect torn about jumping to Web3 — S: distributed architecture + AI delivery experience; W: no smart-contract practice; O: the Web3+AI early window; T: regulation and cash-flow risk; cross: SO pursue a tech-lead-level offer with equity on "architecture + AI", WO learn Solidity on the side, WT negotiate guaranteed cash and clear vesting/exit terms.

**Boundary**: static, unprioritized, easily becomes a list; only a discussion starting point — sort strategies with the decision matrix, don't treat it as the final decision.

**Further reading**: [SWOT analysis — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SWOT_analysis)

### 5 Whys · B

**Logic**: ask "why" repeatedly to descend from the surface symptom to a changeable mechanism layer. A root-cause analysis tool from the Toyota Production System.

**Application**: describe the problem (with data) → ask "why did it happen?" → ask again of the answer → until the answer points to a changeable system element (process, standard, incentive, design) → set a countermeasure and verify.

**Boundary**: five is an empirical number, not mandatory. **The biggest risk is chasing a single line to the end and ignoring multiple-causes-one-effect.** Complex problems should switch to a fishbone diagram or multi-factor analysis. Reaching "people aren't careful enough" usually means you haven't reached the root cause.

**Further reading**: [Five whys — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_whys)

### Systems Thinking · B

**Logic**: many problems "get worse the more you fix them" because we see only events, not structure. Systems thinking looks at three things: reinforcing loops (the more … the more …), balancing loops (self-correction), and delays (today's cause, tomorrow's effect).

**Application**: draw a causal loop diagram → find reinforcing and balancing loops → mark delay links → find leverage points (change structure, not events) → run small experiments and observe long-term feedback.

**Boundary**: system diagrams are easy to draw beautifully but hard to verify. **Without data and time-series support, "systems analysis" is just high-grade storytelling.** It suits retrospectives and long-term strategy, not tactical problems needing a same-day decision.

**Source**: Peter Senge, *The Fifth Discipline*.

### First Principles · B

**Logic**: instead of reasoning by analogy ("everyone does it this way"), break down to irreducible basic facts and rebuild upward from them. Musk's phrasing: "peel away the surface layer by layer to see the essence, then walk back up from the essence."

**Three steps**: clearly define the problem and its essential requirement → deconstruct the basic elements and cost structure → re-derive the solution from the essence, testing multiple possibilities.

**Boundary**: **first principles doesn't end at "seeing the essence" — the point is "rebuilding from the essence".** It's costly, suited to disruptive innovation and cost restructuring, not everyday decisions (where analogy and experience are more efficient). Treating it as "rethink everything from scratch" causes an efficiency disaster.

**Further reading**: [Zhihu: thinking model 03 — First Principles](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/378597541)

### Sky–Rain–Umbrella · C

**Logic**: McKinsey's minimalist decision structure — Sky (facts) → Rain (interpretation) → Umbrella (action). Seeing dark clouds is a fact; judging it will rain is an interpretation; carrying an umbrella is an action.

**Application**: when reporting, give facts first (data) → then interpretation (what this means) → then an action proposal (so do what).

**Boundary**: extremely simple, and therefore easy to degrade into "three-part boilerplate". **Its value is forcing you to separate "fact" from "interpretation"** — most reports fail precisely by mixing the two.

**Source**: McKinsey's "Sky–Rain–Umbrella" thinking framework.

### Golden Circle (Why-How-What) · C

**Logic**: Simon Sinek proposes that inspirational communication starts with "why", not "what". It appeals to identity rather than information.

**Boundary**: suits brand narrative, mobilization, and vision communication; not technical proposal reviews or data reports — those need conclusion and evidence first. **"Always lead with Why" is a misuse.**

**Further reading**: [Start with Why — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Start_with_Why)

### Six Thinking Hats · C

**Logic**: meeting conflict often comes from "using different modes of thinking at the same time". Six hats split thinking into six parallel modes, everyone wearing one at a time: White (facts), Red (emotion), Black (risk), Yellow (value), Green (creativity), Blue (process).

**Boundary**: suits meetings needing multiple perspectives with unequal participant status — protects minority views; not for urgent decisions, nor for small teams already well-aligned (feels bureaucratic).

**Further reading**: [Six Thinking Hats — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Six_Thinking_Hats)

### Analysis Framework Library · B/C

The value of a framework is "saving the work of building a structure from scratch", but **using the wrong framework is more dangerous than using none**.

| Framework | Use | Key reminder |
| --- | --- | --- |
| PEST | Macro-environment scan | Scan only, don't produce strategy |
| 3C | Company / customer / competitor | The three must corroborate each other |
| Porter's Five Forces | Industry attractiveness | For industry analysis, not a single project |
| BCG Matrix | Product portfolio | Define growth and share consistently first |
| Ansoff Matrix | Growth paths | New product + new market is riskiest |
| GROW | Coaching dialogue | Goal-Reality-Options-Will; the point is Will |
| RACI | Responsibility assignment | Each item can have only one A |
| MoSCoW | Requirement priority | Must over 60% means no trade-offs were made |

### Antifragility / Barbell Strategy · C

**Logic**: Taleb argues that rather than predicting black swans, construct a structure that benefits from volatility. The barbell strategy: 85%–90% extremely conservative + 10%–15% extremely aggressive, abandoning the middle.

**Application**: career: "stable main job + high-upside side project"; learning: "solid foundation + a little high-risk exploration"; investing: "cash/bonds + a small position in high volatility".

**Boundary**: this is philosophy and heuristic, not a precisely executable model. Its premise is "downside capped, upside unlimited" — many so-called "side projects" don't satisfy this (the downside is time and health).

**Source**: Nassim Nicholas Taleb, *Antifragile*.

## Combining Methods

A single method solves only one link's problem. The basis of combination is **division of labor by process** — direction, execution, and improvement are each handled by a different method — not "which method is better". The plans below come from cross-analysis of high-like practices on Zhihu, CSDN, and PingCode; **the first three chains have cross-validation from multiple independent sources, the last four are combinations derived from mechanism-based division of labor** — those marked "medium" still lack external cross-validation, so observe for a month before deciding to keep them.

### Combination Principles

1. **Divide by link**: one primary method per link; when two methods solve the same problem, drop one.
2. **Minimal sufficiency**: keep a combination to 3–5 links; add a link only when the current combination exposes that bottleneck.
3. **Evidence grading**: give judgment and verification links to S/A/B methods; C-grade only plays an execution-assist role.
4. **Data validation**: a combination is a hypothesis; verify with completion rate, review data, etc.; if ineffective after a month, adjust the combination rather than stacking methods.

### Chain Quick Reference

| Scenario | Combination chain | Cross-validation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Daily execution | Quadrants → SMART → Pomodoro/time-block → AAR | High (5+ independent sources) |
| Learning a new field | SMART → Feynman + active recall → spaced repetition → interleaving → deliberate practice + feedback | High (3+ sources, mostly S/A components) |
| Complex project | 5W2H → pre-mortem → decision matrix → AAR + PDCA | Medium-high (2–3 sources) |
| Deep output | Energy curve → time blocking → deep work → shutdown ritual → KISS → PDCA | Medium (mechanism clear, no controlled experiment) |
| Knowledge curation | Syntopical reading / SQ3R → atomic notes → active recall → spaced repetition → output → feedback | High (mostly S/A/B components) |
| Problem solving | 5W2H → MECE → 5 Whys → decision matrix → pre-mortem → AAR | High (each has evidence or practice consensus) |
| Long-term capability | Identity → atomic habits → deliberate practice → feedback → SOP → quarterly review | Medium (mostly B/C components) |

### Case 1: Daily Execution Flow

For a developer. Morning 10 minutes: list all tasks → classify with quadrants → fill the judgment criteria for cross-day tasks with SMART → assign 2–3 focus blocks to core tasks (25 or 50 minutes, adjusted by task) → log interruptions into a temporary list, handle after the block → 5-minute AAR in the evening → Sunday PDCA solidifies what worked and adjusts what didn't.

**Key handoff**: quadrants decide "which to pick", Pomodoro only serves already-picked tasks; scheduling Pomodoros for all tasks is formalism.

### Case 2: Learning a New Field

For a technical certification. Week 0 sets the goal with SMART (e.g. "pass in 3 months, 6 hours a week") → every study session starts with active recall: close the material and reproduce the last framework → Feynman self-test section by section during close reading, return to the source when stuck → make Anki cards the same day for fact-type knowledge, handed to spaced repetition → mix question types when practicing (interleaving) → a mock exam every 2 weeks (deliberate practice + feedback), wrong answers locating the weakest sub-skill for the next round.

**Key handoff**: active recall serves both preview (activating old knowledge) and review (retrieval practice); understanding → Feynman, memory → spaced repetition, application → interleaving and deliberate practice — no overlap, no gap.

### Case 3: Complex Project

For a system refactor. Kickoff: 5W2H turns the fuzzy order into a seven-question list (Why decides whether it's worth doing) → compare options with a decision matrix (weights fixed in advance to avoid post-hoc flattery) → pre-mortem pre-installs a risk list and monitoring signals, wired into the task queue rather than left on paper → execute per Case 1 → closeout AAR aligns expectations with reality → conclusions feed PDCA to solidify into standards and roll into the next cycle.

**Key handoff**: the decision matrix compares candidate options; the pre-mortem only pre-rehearses an already-chosen plan — the two are not interchangeable or mergeable.

### Case 4: Deep Output Flow

For writing a technical proposal. The night before, use Top-Three to lock "tomorrow only advances the proposal draft" → in the morning, place the hardest section into 9:00–10:30 by the energy curve → phone silent, messages folded, one document open → single-task inside the block, log a breakpoint instead of switching when stuck → end the block with a 3-minute shutdown ritual (record the breakpoint and next step) → evening KISS with one line of "continue / stop / start" → Sunday PDCA checks whether deep blocks got eroded by meetings this week.

**Key handoff**: **energy management decides "when", time blocking decides "what", deep work decides "how".** The order can't be reversed — scheduling time first and then finding yourself without energy is the most common failure.

### Case 5: Knowledge Curation Flow

For systematically learning a new technical domain. First define the goal as a question ("be able to independently design and explain this architecture's trade-offs") → syntopical read 2–3 classics + SQ3R the official docs → write one atomic note per section (own words + source) → self-test with active recall the same day, return for gaps → make cards for fact-type content into spaced repetition → produce one analysis article or internal share every two weeks → feed readers'/colleagues' follow-up questions back into the notes.

**Key handoff**: **SQ3R gets it "read in", Zettelkasten gets it "stored", active recall gets it "retrieved", output gets it "used".** Missing any link leaves knowledge stuck at "seems like I learned it".

### Case 6: Long-Term Capability Building (Quarterly)

For "improving architecture ability". First fix the identity ("I'm an architect who makes trade-offs under constraints") → design a two-minute minimal action (read a passage of source code daily and write one comment) → attach it to an existing habit (after lunch) → use deliberate practice on the weakest sub-skill (e.g. capacity estimation) → get feedback from a senior colleague every two weeks → write stable, effective practices into a personal SOP → quarterly review: which actions stay, which go.

**Key handoff**: **habits drive "consistency", deliberate practice drives "breakthrough".** Habits without deliberate practice plateau; deliberate practice without habits is three days on, two days off.

### Common Combination Mistakes

| Mistake | Manifestation | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tool replaces method | Lots of time on configuring tools | Run the method first, then solidify into a tool |
| Mechanical application | Pomodoro breaks a deep task's flow | Adjust duration by task nature |
| Collecting replaces practice | Hundreds of saved methodologies, none run through | Run one at a time, 4 weeks each |
| System over-design | Two weeks building Notion, abandoned in week 3 | Run it on paper and the simplest tool first |
| Method stacking | Pomodoro + GTD + OKR + cards all at once | Keep 3–5 links; beyond that it collapses |
| Ignoring boundaries | Using GTD for a three-person team | First ask "who was this method designed for" |
| Learn but never drop | Keep "I already started" even when ineffective | Adjust after a month of ineffectiveness, don't add sunk cost |

### Three Selection Questions

1. **Which link am I stuck on?** Direction (don't know what to do), Execution (can't finish), Improvement (did it but didn't grow), Learning (learned but can't remember), Communication (can't explain) — locate first, then pick.
2. **How much maintenance can I afford?** GTD, Zettelkasten, and Second Brain are heavy systems; if weekly maintenance exceeds 1 hour, you'll likely quit.
3. **How do I know it works?** Fix an observable metric in advance (completion rate, review accuracy, delivery cycle) and check after a month. **A method without a validation plan is just faith.**

### Three Reminders About "Viral Content"

1. **Survivorship bias**: you see "people who succeeded with this method", not "people who failed with the same method".
2. **Misattribution**: a successful person's method may just be a bystander factor; the real variables are resources, timing, and execution.
3. **Narrative preference**: platforms reward "simple, certain, emotional" narratives, while real methods are "conditional, costly, and full of back-and-forth".

Treat viral posts as leads, not conclusions; treat a method as a hypothesis, and validate it with a month of your own data.
